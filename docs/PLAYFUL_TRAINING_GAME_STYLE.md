# Playful training game style

> This file is vendored. Local changes can and will be overwritten by propagation.

Build games people want to play, with teaching that belongs in the experience.
**Fun first, teaching when relevant.** When a teaching device competes with enjoyable play,
redesign it around the action, move it to a natural pause, make it optional, or leave it out.

Use this alongside [FUN_VIBES_DESIGN_STYLE.md](FUN_VIBES_DESIGN_STYLE.md) when building one game
for younger and older players. This guide contributes the older-learner and subject-practice
lens; both audiences benefit from enjoyable action, readable feedback, and expressive artwork.
Start with audience and learning purpose, then use the contents for practice, art, or acceptance.

## Audience and learning purpose

### Scope and shared foundation

Use this guide for science adventures, practice games, protocol puzzles, and arcade trainers.
Learning may be the reason to choose the topic; the moment-to-moment experience still needs an
interesting action, understandable consequences, and a reason to try again.

The original older-learner audience includes older teens, college students, and adults. Consider
their needs alongside younger players' needs in the same game:

- Give practice a recognizable purpose and offer useful depth for players who want it.
- Use direct, respectful explanations that welcome newcomers of any age. Keep advanced context
  available without making it required reading for everyone.
- Offer hints and optional challenge according to the player's needs and experience. Age alone
  does not establish subject knowledge, reading confidence, or comfort with game controls.
- Review the shared opening for accessibility to newcomers and later play for continued interest
  among experienced players. Let both audiences enjoy the same world, humor, and spectacle.

[FUN_VIBES_DESIGN_STYLE.md](FUN_VIBES_DESIGN_STYLE.md) owns the shared guidance on game feel,
progression, recovery, expressive artwork, rewards, and design iteration. It also records the
four book sources and six local game examples behind this revision. Apply that foundation here.

Apply [expect the unexpected](FUN_VIBES_DESIGN_STYLE.md#expect-the-unexpected) throughout play.
Keep new encounters, discoveries, helpful surprises, and visible payoffs arriving regularly.
A surprise can earn its place through enjoyment alone; it need not introduce another lesson.

Choose tone for the actual experience. Older learners can enjoy cute characters, strange cells,
loud celebrations, and bright colors. A restrained professional trainer is one useful direction;
it is not the required appearance of every game with educational content.

### Choose what play teaches

State the enjoyable action first, then name any understanding it can usefully develop. Keep
these three approaches distinct:

| Approach | What the player does | Appropriate learning claim |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Subject reasoning | Uses a substrate to choose an enzyme or manages reaction inputs | Practices the represented relationship |
| Process adventure | Moves through binding, transport, and a visible final transformation | Encounters a simplified process and its order |
| Themed arcade | Dodges, shoots, or chases in a biological setting | Builds interest and familiarity; optional context adds detail |

A themed arcade is a valid outcome. It does not need quiz interruptions to justify its existence.
Subject reasoning should offer a satisfying decision, and a process adventure should reward
exploration rather than become a slow illustrated lecture.

- Identify the relationship the player should notice, if there is one: input and product,
  structure and function, cause and consequence, or a meaningful dependency.
- Put that relationship into the action where it fits. Let reactions change the inventory,
  binding change the character's available actions, or a successful repair change the world.
- Use familiar controls to free attention for the new situation. Add complexity through the
  environment or decisions before expanding the control scheme.
- Keep explanations available at the moment of curiosity: a short contextual line, optional
  inspect action, pause panel, or end-of-stage reflection.
- If a lesson repeatedly stops a good loop, change its delivery. Preserve the playable promise.

### Keep the model honest

Use playful simplification while preserving the specific relationship the game claims to teach.
Record which parts are subject rules, which are simplifications, and which are arcade inventions.
Give players a short explanation at a useful point when a metaphor could create a misconception.

- Preserve important causal dependencies. If binding enables recognition, make that change
  observable; let an incomplete destination show what remains to do.
- Treat quantities as meaningful when the learning goal depends on them. Distinguish resources
  spent, products made, and net gain instead of collapsing them into a generic score.
- Use accurate subject labels and characteristic shapes while allowing exaggerated scale,
  expressions, motion, and environments.
- Keep decorative science from implying false mechanisms. A bouncing organelle or plaque-splitting
  weapon can be an enjoyable invention with a clearly stated limit.
- Verify subject claims against the project's domain sources. A successful game-design pattern
  does not by itself validate the science.

For example, glycolysis-okimon's reaction ledger distinguishes ATP invested, ATP produced, and
net ATP. Plaque Attack describes its tools as arcade metaphors. Those are different but coherent
contracts with the player.

### Professional trainer option

When the requested experience is a realistic onboarding trainer, use authentic decisions,
credible consequences, and concise explanations. Calm copy and a clean work surface may suit that
setting; choose them because they help the scenario, rather than because adults require restraint.

Possible interactions include ordering protocol steps, spotting a mistake in a scene, choosing a
tool for a job, or exploring a branching response. Pick the interaction that makes the decision
interesting. Each has its own feedback and state needs; they need not share a multiple-choice loop.

Give scenarios enough context to be fair. Base explanations on the applicable procedure and show
the learner why a choice matters. If a workflow has formal assessment requirements, keep those
requirements explicit and distinguish the game from the assessment.

## Practice and feedback

### Design practice worth repeating

Koster's mastery lens suggests asking what the player is learning to do, including game skills.
For subject practice, vary the situation enough that the player uses the relationship again.

- Introduce one unfamiliar relationship in a forgiving encounter, then reuse it in a changed
  context. Combine familiar ideas once players have had a chance to recognize them.
- Change substrates, constraints, available resources, or partner roles. Reordered labels alone
  may leave the same shortcut intact.
- Check whether players can succeed by always choosing the newest recruit, the same screen
  position, or the longest answer. When reasoning matters, remove that shortcut at its source.
- Let optional exploration add humor, curiosity, and useful context without blocking the main
  journey or creating collection chores.
- Revisit a missed concept later when that serves practice. Keep the next encounter interesting
  instead of forcing immediate repetition until a counter fills.
- Let short games reach a satisfying ending. Replay can come from a new approach or a shared
  experience; daily engagement systems are optional.

### Make mistakes useful

A mistake should leave the player with a useful next action. Give specific, neutral feedback
without turning every miss into an interruption.

#### Choice and scenario feedback

For a wrong answer in a quiz or explicit reasoning encounter, show:

1. **What the learner chose.** Keep the selection visible.
2. **What works.** Show the correction, or a helpful hint followed by an accessible explanation
   when the encounter is built around discovery.
3. **Why it matters.** Connect the choice to the relevant relationship or consequence.

For example:

> Your enzyme acts on a different substrate. Compare its substrate with the molecule at this gate,
> then choose a matching enzyme. You can inspect your party before trying again.

For a direct-answer trainer, name the correct choice explicitly alongside that explanation.
Use common misunderstandings as distractors, keep the answer unambiguous, and show enough context
to reason from. Let the learner control reading time with a clear Continue or Try again action.

Keep the chosen and corrected labels readable. Corner badges and outlines can mark feedback
without changing colors that already identify characters, resources, or choice slots.

#### Action and exploration feedback

For a missed jump, mistimed shot, or failed docking attempt:

- Make the cause visible through motion, contact, a brief cue, or the changed state of the world.
- Restore control quickly in a usable place. Preserve discoveries or completed encounters when
  replaying them would add only repetition.
- Offer a hint after repeated difficulty, or let the player request one. Keep it tied to the
  current obstacle rather than delivering a general lecture.
- Allow safe experimentation and fair consequences. A failure can be funny or dramatic when the
  cause is understandable and another attempt is inviting.

## Artwork and rewards

### Use art to invite curiosity

Follow the shared
[FUN_VIBES_DESIGN_STYLE.md](FUN_VIBES_DESIGN_STYLE.md#give-the-world-character) artwork guidance
and use `vosslab-skills:svg-creator-expert` when available for editable SVG creation and review.

Art can make the world inviting through beauty, humor, strangeness, and satisfying movement.
Give it that role alongside teaching. The shared guide covers tangible settings, character
identity, visible transformations, and concise briefs that leave room for invention.

- Let the subject suggest unusual silhouettes and environments: folded membranes, branching
  tissue, molecular scaffolds, crowded cargo, or well-used lab equipment.
- Give characters a personality through proportion, pose, expression, and behavior. Let their
  recognizable subject features survive the exaggeration.
- Make the world feel inhabited through purposeful irregularity, wear, growth, repairs, and
  traces of earlier activity. Keep important openings, resources, and hazards easy to identify.
- Show cause and consequence in the artwork: cargo fills a carrier, a reaction changes the pack,
  a receptor binds, or a damaged region recovers.
- Make accomplishment change something the player cares about. Compare beginning, middle, and
  completed states at gameplay size; preserve the subject's identity while making change obvious
  without relying on a progress counter.
- Use shared contours, lighting, and material treatment to make the cast belong together.
  Keep secondary detail quieter than the action.
- Judge the asset at actual gameplay size in its real scene. A beautiful molecular illustration
  still needs a readable silhouette and a useful action pose.

Keep required directions short and concrete. Let the illustrations carry atmosphere and spatial
meaning while optional captions provide deeper explanation.

### Reward play and progress

- Make successful actions, new abilities, visible world changes, and discoveries the first rewards.
- Add badges, scores, streaks, or cosmetics when they reinforce the experience. A reward economy
  is optional; several subject resources can coexist when each has an understandable use.
- Distinguish completion, assistance used, and demonstrated reasoning where that distinction helps
  the player. Keep hints inviting rather than treating curiosity as failure.
- Describe in-game achievements precisely: "Completed the enzyme challenges" or "Practiced these
  scenarios". Reserve claims of professional readiness or verified competence for evidence that
  supports them; a badge earned in play does not establish those claims.
- Make progress screens useful for choosing the next activity. Keep menus and statistics from
  displacing the game itself.

## Review and acceptance

### Review the whole experience

Use Fullerton's prototype-and-observe approach and Schell's experience and interest lenses from
the shared [FUN_VIBES_DESIGN_STYLE.md](FUN_VIBES_DESIGN_STYLE.md#book-sources-and-applications)
book sources.

Follow the shared [independent completion guidance](FUN_VIBES_DESIGN_STYLE.md#plan-for-independent-completion).
Every milestone must be completable by the manager and subagents while the human is unavailable.
Use independent agent assessments, automated behavior tests, and rendered evidence for acceptance.
Learner sessions, instructor feedback, and human art reviews are optional refinement inputs.

Save final named screenshots as WebP at quality 90 (`cwebp -q 90`), following `docs/REPO_STYLE.md`.
Inspect text, feedback, and artwork in the final WebP; use `.webp` filenames in reports and docs.

- Describe the enjoyable experience the opening should deliver, and observe whether it does.
- Look for a satisfying cycle of anticipation, action, consequence, and another worthwhile choice.
- Inspect early, middle, and late encounters for variation, pressure, recovery, and a final payoff.
- Check whether a teaching interruption improves the current experience. Shorten, relocate, or
  remove it when it breaks momentum without adding useful understanding.
- For subject reasoning, test changed examples to verify that the game applies the represented
  relationship consistently. Assess learner transfer during optional learner sessions; a
  remembered answer, agent solution, or completed route alone does not establish understanding.
- Separate source inspection, automated completion, visual captures, player reactions, and
  learning evidence in reports. Use the evidence available and state its limits.

### Design review checklist

- [ ] The main action is enjoyable and understandable before explanations are added.
- [ ] Younger and older players can share the core game, with accessible entry and optional depth.
- [ ] Teaching is relevant to the current action or available at a natural pause.
- [ ] The game is clear about subject rules, simplifications, and arcade inventions.
- [ ] Practice varies meaningful decisions when reasoning is the intended skill.
- [ ] Feedback explains the useful next action and makes retry inviting.
- [ ] Characters and environments feel distinctive, inhabited, and readable during play.
- [ ] Progress and the ending give the player a visible payoff.
- [ ] Learning claims match the evidence; rewards describe what the player actually did.
- [ ] The entire plan has an agent-executable completion path without human interaction gates.

Review findings should name a concrete failure of the playable promise, clarity, fairness,
accessibility, or subject accuracy. Bright colors, cute characters, and exuberant motion are
welcome when they work. **Fun first, teaching when relevant** remains the deciding priority.

## Examples and references

### Borrow concrete strengths

The source examples are described and linked in
[FUN_VIBES_DESIGN_STYLE.md](FUN_VIBES_DESIGN_STYLE.md#learn-from-these-games).

- **glycolysis-okimon** makes ATP investment, products, and enzyme choice part of the route.
  Shuffled substrates give recruited enzymes a purpose beyond collection. Borrow the connection
  between a useful game resource and an understandable subject relationship.
- **Steroid Quest** gives a cellular journey varied movement and a visible transcription payoff.
  Borrow its recurring "what strange thing comes next?" promise, a sequence that players enact,
  stable controls, and recovery that preserves discovery.
- **Ms Taq Man** turns movement into a growing DNA trail. Borrow the visible transformation and
  memorable molecular cast while keeping its chase rules separate from biological claims.
- **Plaque Attack** makes shooting tactically interesting and campaign progress visibly restorative.
  Borrow the action-and-payoff structure; its weapons and healing are explicitly arcade metaphors.
- **Bad Cell Day** makes characters and their asymmetric interactions central to cooperation.
  Borrow roles that matter during play. A molecular cast can be memorable without stopping every
  puzzle for an explanation.
- **Super Bowling** shows that anticipation, physical consequence, and spectacle can sustain an
  experience. A learning game deserves the same care for feel and payoff as a recreational game.

These are design inferences from the selected games, not evidence that a particular mechanic
produces learning gains. Enjoyment, completion, and subject understanding are different outcomes.
