# Organon: periodic-grid

A saturated chart with the *expectation* that every cell is filled. Empty cells are not "missing data" — they are predictions that something should exist with those properties. Named for Mendeleev's periodic table, which famously predicted gallium, germanium, and scandium by their gaps.

## To periodic-grid-ify an idea

1. **Pick two ordered dimensions** that, in combination, are expected to enumerate every instance of a domain. The combination must feel exhaustive — every reasonable pairing of a row-value and column-value should correspond to an actually-existing or genuinely-missing instance.
2. **Lay out the grid and populate from known instances.** Fill every cell you can with named, known instances.
3. **Mark each empty cell explicitly as a prediction.** What instance, if it existed, would live here? What properties would it have, derived from its row and column?
4. **Examine the predictions.** Some will be impossible (the row and column properties contradict). Some will be already-existing instances you simply hadn't placed. Some will be genuine candidates for invention.

## What periodic-grids surface

- **Predicted instances that don't exist yet** — the periodic table's signature payoff. The grid says they *should* exist; sometimes they do exist and you find them; sometimes they don't and you've found a coining opportunity or a structural impossibility.
- **Apparent contradictions that aren't** — an empty cell that "shouldn't" be empty often reveals a hidden assumption you'd been making about why it couldn't be filled.
- **Saturation as a discipline** — the *expectation* of completeness is what distinguishes this organon from a regular chart, and what makes its empties productive rather than merely descriptive.

## Pairing

Periodic-grids pair with **dimension-identification** (the two axes must be load-bearing dimensions that exhaust the space) and with **substitution** (each cell is a substitution-variant of the others, varying along one or both axes). They're the right organon when you suspect the idea-space is *complete enough* that absence is informative.
