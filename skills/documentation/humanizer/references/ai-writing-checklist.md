# AI-writing review checklist

Use this checklist for substantive rewrites, voice matching, or a requested review of AI-like
prose. For a short email or a small copyedit, use only the checks that address the draft. Check
meaning and voice even when the wording looks polished. Mark a check complete when reviewed,
not when every matching phrase is removed. These are editorial prompts, not evidence of
who wrote a passage, banned phrases, or targets for an AI detector.

## Language and claims

- [ ] C01. Filler openings. Start with the claim instead of "It is important to note that."
  Use "to" or "because" when it preserves the meaning. Keep a signpost that points readers to
  a condition or section they need.
- [ ] C02. Abstract praise words. Replace vague uses of "leverage," "robust," or "seamless"
  with the actual operation or property. Keep established terms such as robust estimation.
- [ ] C03. Inflated importance. Replace "pivotal moment" or "plays a crucial role" with what
  happened or changed. Keep a significance claim if the passage supports its scope.
- [ ] C04. Vague authority. Replace "research shows" or "experts agree" with the actual source
  and what it found. Flag unverified attribution rather than dropping it and asserting the claim.
- [ ] C05. Decorative analysis. Ask what "highlighting its potential" adds. Cut it if it only
  praises the previous sentence; keep a clause that explains a supported effect or operation.
- [ ] C06. Roundabout verbs. Use "is" or "has" when equal in meaning to "stands as" or
  "boasts." Keep "serves as" when role, mapping, or temporary function differs from identity.
  Don't expand a narrow phrase list into a frequency limit for similar-looking verbs.
- [ ] C07. Synonym cycling. Keep the precise name for the same object rather than rotating
  "tool," "platform," and "solution." Keep distinct labels for distinct things; there is no
  target vocabulary diversity, and forced synonyms can erase a technical distinction.
- [ ] C17. Decorative word upgrades. Use a plain exact word in place of a more formal one when
  it says the same thing. Keep technical words that carry a distinction the shorter word loses.
- [ ] C18. Template-first research introductions. Check whether the background, gap, and
  purpose follow the study's reasoning. Keep a real gap or reader roadmap; skip empty slots.

## Rhetoric and repetition

- [ ] C08. Repeated binary contrasts. Keep "not X but Y" if the distinction matters. Drop a
  mirrored contrast that invents an opposition or only restates the point.
- [ ] C09. Forced lists and false ranges. List the cases or steps that exist. Keep a triad
  with three real items and a "from X to Y" range with meaningful endpoints.
- [ ] C10. Document narration. Start with the substance instead of "Let's dive in" or a
  sentence that echoes its heading. Keep orientation in a long or technical section if needed.
- [ ] C11. Restatement without progress. Keep a rephrasing that teaches a difficult point or
  adds a new definition, example, or implication. Cut one that only repeats the claim.
- [ ] C12. Interchangeable paragraphs. Order paragraphs by the reasoning and let length follow
  the content. Keep useful parallel methods, glossary entries, or deliberate repetition; avoid
  imposing rhythm quotas or varying a structure just because it repeats.
- [ ] C13. Manufactured suspense or candor. State the information directly instead of "The
  catch?" or "The honest answer." Keep a question that helps teach and retain real disclosures.

## Presentation and endings

- [ ] C14. Mechanical formatting. Review repeated dash asides, bold labels, excess headings,
  and decorative emoji in context. Follow the author's punctuation and destination style. Check
  the original layout before cutting a separator; retain correct hyphenation and useful lists.
- [ ] C15. Slogan endings. End with a supported result, limitation, or next action. Keep a
  memorable closing if it expresses the actual conclusion; stop when there's nothing to add.
- [ ] C16. Chatbot or draft residue. Remove canned pleasantries and process announcements from
  the draft. Resolve placeholders and citation tokens; verify the references behind them.

## Final meaning and voice

- [ ] C19. Claim and edit-scope drift. Compare the accepted version with the author's original,
  not only the previous rewrite. Check what is asserted, who did it, who said it, numbers and
  units, negation, causal direction, scope, conditions, and whether the evidence still warrants
  the same degree of certainty. Check both overstatement and over-softening: keep warranted
  hedges and supported categorical claims. Check the whole draft for edits outside the requested
  passage. Verify that the citation still serves its intended role: support, contrast, or mention.
  Verify added ideas and causal links separately from wording edits; restore the intended claim
  or obtain author approval for substantive changes.
- [ ] C20. Voice and stance drift. Compare the intended viewpoint, certainty, affect, role,
  degree of formality, and reader relationship, not only factual equivalence. Use the author's
  drafts or accepted edits in the same genre first. Preserve deliberate first person, politeness,
  calibrated qualification, and useful passives. Keep a supported correction or change of tone
  the author wants for the audience; don't mistake fluency or reader approval for preserved voice.

For Dr. Voss's technical writing, start with the concrete problem, name the measurement or
criterion, and say what follows and where the conclusion stops. The publication-based analysis
is a secondary reference: coauthored papers cannot always establish individual phrasing. For
student emails, follow his own email drafts and explicit preferences, including `5pm`. Preserve
what has or hasn't been assigned; don't turn a possible lesson into a promised lesson or an
optional request into an overdue requirement.

## Worked checks

These examples illustrate editing decisions; they are not quotations or detector tests.

- C01/C06: "It is important to note that the software serves as a tool for exporting questions" can
  become "The software exports questions" if export is an actual capability. Don't add a format.
- C08: Keep "Biological validity does not establish equal item difficulty." The distinction is
  the point, not filler.
- C19: "The results could potentially suggest that the treatment may reduce growth in this sample"
  can become "The results suggest that the treatment may reduce growth in this sample." Keeping
  "may" and "in this sample" preserves the tentative, sample-bounded conclusion.
- C19/C20: "If you've started, bring what you have" is not equivalent to "Bring the work you've
  completed." The second version makes progress sound mandatory.
