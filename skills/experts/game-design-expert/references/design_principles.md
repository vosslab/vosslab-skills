# Shape play and surprise

Use these principles to make decisions about the target game. Concrete examples
and their limits live in [case_studies.md](case_studies.md); books support the
reasoning through [reference_survey.md](reference_survey.md).

## Make the action satisfying

- Tune the main verb in an actual encounter. Examine input acceptance, response,
  acceleration, stopping, collision, camera, and available space together.
- Match visible contact with collision behavior. Buffered turns and forgiving
  input windows can express intent while preserving meaningful challenge.
- Make actions change something understandable: a route, resource, partner's
  opportunity, hazard, or world state. Show why a choice matters before commitment.
- Let sound, animation, particles, and camera reinforce the actual event. Preserve
  readable controls and hazards during spectacle; scale emphasis with consequence.
- Keep failure legible and retries quick. Review whether a loss teaches a usable
  response; distinguish intended difficulty from an input or collision defect.

## Expect the unexpected

Keep players wondering what strange, delightful, or useful thing comes next.
Surprise is valuable in its own right. New situations can create humor, beauty,
curiosity, relief, or advantage as well as challenge.

- Introduce a distinctive interaction early. Give each region a signature moment
  and keep discoveries arriving within regions too.
- Teach reliable rules, then combine them unexpectedly: ride an enemy attack,
  turn cover into a firing lane, or use a familiar current to find a shortcut.
- Vary mechanics, spatial problems, environments, enemy behavior, scale, allies,
  rewards, and discoveries. Strong existing mechanics can support fresh situations.
- Alternate discovery, practice, recombination, pressure, payoff, and breathing
  room. Let a surprise register before the next arrives; judge cadence from play.
- Audit stretches of already-solved repetition. Add a new decision, interaction,
  sight, or payoff where the experience grows stale. Keep worthwhile practice.
- Track novelty separately from difficulty. More speed, health, enemies, or travel
  can increase demands without creating a fresh experience.
- Introduce new rules with enough space and readable cues to respond. Keep controls
  dependable, outcomes understandable afterward, and recovery accessible.
- Use authored encounters and systemic combinations where they fit. Surprise
  needs neither random punishment nor a universal timer or new-button quota.

## Progression and meaningful rewards

Make progress visible through unlocked possibilities, growing competence, changed
relationships, and a transformed world. A recovered patient or huge pin cascade
can supply a stronger payoff than another number. Tie celebration to what happened.

For cooperative games, give partners useful differences and reciprocal dependence.
For puzzles, separate discovering the solution from executing it. Introduce tools
with reduced competing pressure, then combine them in richer situations. Preserve
both satisfying mastery and curiosity about what follows.

## Learning that belongs in play

State the enjoyable action first. When teaching is relevant, map a subject
relationship to player decisions: consume reaction inputs, choose an enzyme for
a substrate, or follow a meaningful sequence. Use contextual feedback that explains
the relationship when the player needs it. Offer deeper reading at natural pauses.

Distinguish scientific representation from arcade abstraction. A themed chase may
invite interest without practicing molecular reasoning. Use optional challenge,
hints, and explanations for different needs; choose presentation from the game's
promise rather than a presumed age-based palette. Rewards, shops, quizzes, touch
controls, persistence, and audio defaults are project choices.
