# Organon-construction

Capture the idea as a structured artifact — an *organon*. The structure forces articulation: empty cells, missing branches, and asymmetric distributions all become visible the moment the structure is filled in.

## Procedure

1. **Pick an organon type.** From the catalog: list, chart, graph, atlas, scale, dictionary, tree. Each has a different grammar and surfaces different gaps. (See `methods/organons/` for the bestiary.)
2. **Populate the structure.** Fit the idea into the chosen form. A list demands enumeration; a chart demands two dimensions and cell-filling; a scale demands an axis and a position; a tree demands a root and recursive decomposition.
3. **Read the result for gaps.** Where is the structure thin? Where are cells empty? Where do you have to invent a name because there's no obvious one? Each gap is a seed for further expansion — apply other operators to those gaps.

## Why organons are the foundation

Per Grace, organons are "artifacts capturing ideas: lists, charts, graphs, atlases, scales, dictionaries." They're the basic units of ideonomic work because:

- An idea floating in your head is hard to operate on. An idea captured in an organon can be combined, sliced, substituted, and negated.
- The act of fitting an idea into a structure already constitutes analysis — you have to decide what counts as an item, what counts as a dimension, where this instance lives.
- Two organons of the same idea (e.g., a *list* of trees and a *chart* of trees by-naturalness × by-longevity) reveal different things. Producing more than one organon of the same idea is itself an expansion technique.

## Pairing with other operators

Organon-construction usually comes first or last in a method tuple:

- **First** — capture the idea, then operate on the captured form.
- **Last** — operate first; organize the resulting expansions into a structure that lets the user see the whole space.
