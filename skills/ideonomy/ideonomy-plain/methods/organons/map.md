# Organon: map

A 2D spatial layout where *position itself* carries information. Distinct from a chart because chart positions are dimensional values; distinct from a graph because graphs are topological (edges matter, position doesn't). Map positions encode adjacency, distance, region, and density.

## To map-ify an idea

1. **Pick a spatial metaphor.** Subway map (route topology), mind map (radial association), concept map (labeled-edge graph laid out spatially), choropleth (regions colored by a value), territory map (named lands with borders).
2. **Lay out items.** Place related items near each other; unrelated items far apart. Distance is meaningful.
3. **Mark regions and borders.** Regions are clusters; borders are where the character changes. Both are organizing constructs the map makes visible.
4. **Read the geography.** Where are the cities (high-density regions)? The badlands (sparse, dangerous)? The contested borders? The peninsulas and islands? The metaphor often surfaces real properties.

## What maps surface

- **Regions you didn't know existed** — a cluster of related items the map's layout makes visible, but a list would scatter.
- **Border zones** — the most interesting ideas often live where two regions meet, and only a map shows them.
- **Empty territory** — the unmapped regions are candidates for exploration. Atlas pages, by contrast, describe the same territory; map empties are *unknown*.

## Pairing

Maps pair with **atlas** (a map is one page of an atlas) and with **cross-domain re-instantiation** (map metaphors travel well — most domains have their own version of "border zone," "capital city," "frontier"). They're the right organon when spatial intuition adds something a list or chart can't — when adjacency and density are themselves informative.
